A parallel character that provides contrast (e.g. different responses to guilt, duty, honor) and emphasizes distinct traits of another (Hamlet's intellectual paralysis).

### How Shakespeare Uses It
A foil is used here primarily to isolate [[Hamlet]]'s paralysis. Both [[Fortinbras]] and [[Laertes]] offer contrasting traits, choices, and temperaments. This highlights Hamlet's defining qualities. Since his internal conflict is so abstract, it is really helpful to see him surrounded with young men facing parallel crises. This forces the audience to judge [[action vs inaction]], having been provided concrete alternatives.

Also, a foil helps provide a more well-rounded profile of the "avenging son". Shakespeare splits this archetype among three figures, essentially proving that neither pure thought nor blind action offers a clean moral exit.

### Examples
- **[[Laertes]] (Impulse):** Like Hamlet, Laertes loses a father to murder (and a sister to tragedy, *see* [[Ophelia's descent]]). Unlike Hamlet, he storms Elsinore immediately (with an angry mob nonetheless), ready to slit Hamlet’s throat in a church. This impulsive fury emphasizes Hamlet’s over-intellectualization, while also proving how easily unthinking rage is manipulated (by [[Claudius]]).
- **[[Fortinbras]] (Discipline, Ambition):** Another prince with a dead father (courtesy of Old Hamlet). Fortinbras channels his energy outward into decisive military action, and it is seeing him risk twenty thousand lives for some worthless Polish land that shames Hamlet into committing to his own vengeance. (*see* [[Fortinbras' troops]])
- **[[Horatio]] (Rationality):** Hamlet is volatile, theatrical, and torn apart by emotion. Horatio is steady, unpretentious, and immune to life's ups and downs. This is a psychological equilibrium Hamlet lacks.
