Theatre is probably the only place you can speak long monologues to yourself without seeming crazy. Argue with yourself and air your dirty laundry (hesitations, fears, conscience).

### How Shakespeare Uses It
It's no secret you must trust no one in Elsinore (*see* [[appearance vs reality]]). That is, unless you're [[Hamlet]] and you have a best friend of utmost[[ loyalty]] (named [[Horatio]]). Even then, melodramatic and angsty as you are, you'll probably still want to give **seven** lengthy speeches while alone on stage to voice your unfiltered internal thoughts (seems pretty excessive, but to each their own). These soliloquies serve as the primary structural spine tracking Hamlet's psychological erosion and growth across the play (one could argue he recovers, but overcoming loss is a different matter from fatalistic acceptance).

These long and dramatic monologues don't only reveal plot plans and character development though, it also significantly dramatizes thought. The audience sees Hamlet arguing himself into fury, talking himself out of action, and testing the waters of his philosophical rumination.

### Examples
- **In the [[First Court Scene]] (Act 1, Scene 2):** Hamlet says in his first soliloquy, *"O, that this too, too sullied flesh would melt"*. We haven't even gotten into the meat of the play. Before it all, Hamlet begins with a lengthy speech outlining his suicidal despair and disgust with [[Gertrude]]'s remarriage. So he was like this even before he learns of his father's murder during the [[ghost's appearance]].
- **After Watching the Players Perform (Act 2, Scene 2):** *"O, what a rogue and peasant slave am I!"* is how Hamlet begins this soliloquy. As he watches a player weep while describing Hecuba's immense [[grief]], he begins to intensely rebuke himself for his own inaction (*see* [[action vs inaction]]). Hamlet's guilt over his own lack of motive and courage, along with amazement over a mere [[performance]]'s ability to move an audience, drives him to stage the [[mousetrap]] (*"The play's the thing / Wherein I'll catch the conscience of the king"*).
- **"[[To be, or not to be]]" (Act 3, Scene 1):** Probably the most famous Shakespeare line ever. Hamlet is now beyond personal revenge (*see* [[revenge vs justice]]), and has devolved (or rather, evolved?) into a universal existential inquiry. He investigates suicide and suffering, and his contemplation culminates in the conclusion that conscience paralyzes decisive action. (*see* [[existentialism]])
- **"O, my offence is rank" (Act 3, Scene 3):** Another character for a change, everyone welcome [[Claudius]]! His sole soliloquy exposes the dilemma that has trapped him. He desperately wants divine forgiveness, but can only obtain such after repentance. Because he refuses to give up his crown and queen, he will never find redemption. (*see* [[corruption]])
- **[[Fortinbras' Troops]] (Act 4, Scene 4):** Back to our incessant whiner, *"How all occasions do inform against me"* is the opening of Hamlet’s final soliloquy. Finally gaining a shred of self-awareness (I'd say emotional maturity, but that might be a stretch), he diagnoses his own fatal flaw (*"thinking too precisely on th' event"*). Taking on a truly noble mindset, Hamlet resolves to let his thoughts be bloody from this point forward.
