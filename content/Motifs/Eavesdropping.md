Elsinore seems to have an unhealthy culture of surveillance: people prefer spying over honest confrontation. Hey, hiding behind a curtain (or arras) might just get you killed. But what do I know.

### Chronological Development
Elsinore opens in the very first act as a surveillance state, one where paranoia drives characters (specifically one above all else) to spy on their own blood relatives. The motif begins when [[Polonius]] sends Reynaldo to Paris to slander Laertes (his own son, by the way). His intent with this is to trick [[Laertes]]'s acquaintances into revealing his vices (*"bait of falsehood"* to catch a *"carp of truth"*). Later, in Act 2, Scene 2, we get to see the monarchs try their hand at eavesdropping. [[Claudius]] and [[Gertrude]] summon [[Rosencrantz & Guildenstern]] to *"glean"* what's making [[Hamlet]] go nuts (under the guise of childhood friendship). Polonius also volunteers to *"loose my daughter to him"* while he hides behind an arras (I know, how noble) so he and the ~~king~~ big C can spy on the encounter (I mean, seems foolproof to me).

Clearly these people haven't had enough (though how much further can you go after selling out your own daughter?). In Act 3, Claudius and Polonius hide behind a tapestry to audit Hamlet's confrontation with Ophelia (as planned). Hamlet likely detects their presence (probably heard something or spotted a lump), as his rhetoric turns violent and cruel (not as planned). In the next scene, it's Hamlet's turn to play spy, as he sets up the [[mousetrap]] and recruits Horatio to monitor Claudius's facial expressions for a flinch of guilt (*"Even with the very comment of thy soul / Observe my uncle"*). Two scenes later (Act 3, Scene 4) Polonius hides behind the arras in Gertrude's closet to spy on a private mother-son confrontation. He pays for his nosy meddling with his life when Hamlet stabs blindly through the curtain, mistaking the eavesdropper for Claudius.

Now we're in the last two acts of the play, and hiding behind curtains are for rookies, so we're gonna graduate to intercepted letters and double-crosses. Claudius calls up ~~Thing 1 & Thing 2~~ Rosencrantz & Guildenstern and gives them sealed execution orders for England. Hamlet then finds the letter and secretly unseals their commission, rewrites it, and reseals it using his father's signet ring. This reverses the covert manipulation and sends Rosencrantz & Guildenstern to their deaths. That's the last notable instance of weaseling intel in the play, as the final events shift to concluding psychological arcs and playing out schemes made in secret (*see* [[the duel]]).

### Who & Where
- **[[Polonius]]** wins the title of obsessive architect by a landslide. Our chief of domestic surveillance is constantly hiding behind tapestries and orchestrating traps in the main lobby. He even goes as far as to hide in the Queen's private bedchamber (just for some tea, unbelievable).
- **[[Claudius]]** is without a doubt second in the race to turn privacy into a myth. He institutionalizes espionage as statecraft, deploying Rosencrantz and Guildenstern across Elsinore’s corridors to do his dirty work. He is also found conspiring behind the arras with his partner in crime, Polonius, to assess whether Hamlet threatens his crown.
- **[[Hamlet]]** and **[[Horatio]]** team up to conduct counter-surveillance in the great hall for their [[mousetrap]] plot. They turn theater itself into an observation trap, though this may be the only necessary use of eavesdropping in the play.

### Connection to Deeper Themes
- **[[Appearance vs Reality]]:** Eavesdropping is a major factor in what makes genuine human intimacy impossible at Elsinore, since any room might be concealing unwelcome listeners. This constant surveillance is what forces every character to put on a [[performance]] for their public role. 
- **[[Certainty & Truth]]:** This one's circular. Direct speech is infested with lies due to [[corruption]] and thus cannot be trusted, so characters resort to spying, which causes more dishonesty. Ironically, none of this yields real clarity anyway. For example, Polonius misreads Hamlet in the [[nunnery scene]], then dies behind a curtain three scenes later. (*see* [[death of Polonius]])
- **[[Isolation]]:** Even your childhood friends (Rosencrantz & Guildenstern) and lover (Ophelia) have been weaponized as informants. Have fun being cut off from every human tie you've ever had (except Horatio).
