Moral rot is spreading like a literal disease at Elsinore. Seems like it may be coming from the throne... this degeneracy (and debauchery) is concerningly pervasive, anyhow.

### Chronological Development
Before we even meet any main characters, before the plot even begins moving, Shakespeare introduces imagery of physical and moral decay. In the first scene of the play, Francisco says he is *"sick at heart*", immediately establishing an atmosphere of unease. This crystallizes later in Act 1 when Marcellus watches [[Hamlet]] follow the ghost into the dark and declares, *"Something is rotten in the state of Denmark"*. As Hamlet is talking to his dead father, [[the ghost]] literally describes how    [[Claudius]]'s [[poison]] curdles his blood and crusts into this leprosy-like texture *"most lazar-like, with vile and loathsome crust."* Hmm, all this physical imagery of rot and decay surely won't fester into political and moral corruption, right?

\[Intermission. In the Elizabethan worldview, the state was viewed as a living organism (weird, I know). The monarch was the head of the "body politic". So when Claudius decides to go and murder his brother, it's beyond some personal crime. He literally corrupts the cosmic and psychological root of Denmark.\]

After Act 1, corruption shifts from imagery of moral decay and references to political crime into an existential obsession within Hamlet's mind (*see* [[existentialism]]). Across Acts 2 and 3, this motif manifests mainly in Hamlet's confrontations. First, when talking to [[Rosencrantz & Guildenstern]] in Act 2, Scene 2, he describes the earth as *"a foul and pestilent congregation of vapors"*, projecting his disgust with Elsinore onto the entire physical universe. Later, when Claudius admits his guilt in his prayer [[soliloquy]], saying that his *"offence is rank, it smells to heaven"*, he is essentially saying his hidden sin emits a spiritual stench so strong it transcends Earth and reaches Heaven. This stench, of course, will follow him so long as he retains his crown. Lastly, when Hamlet confronts [[Gertrude]], he begs her to stop laying a *"flattering unction"* to her soul. He continues with *"will but skin and film the ulcerous place, / Whiles rank corruption, mining all within, / Infects unseen."* Hamlet is telling his mother to stop putting band-aids and ointment on deep, infected wounds because it will only cause the infection to tunnel deeper.

By Acts 4 and 5, the corruption has erupts beyond diplomatic facade and is now public. Claudius attempts to excuse Hamlet's murder of [[Polonius]] by calling him *"the owner of a foul disease"* (referencing his [[madness]]), one that Claudius keeps secret until *"it feed even on the pith of life"*. Ironic he says this about Hamlet, though, because he's the one hiding his regicide until the moral rot hollows out the kingdom and kills everyone. Also, Hamlet mocks Polonius’s corpse as a *"convocation of politic worms"* (*see* [[death of Polonius]]). Finally, in the [[gravedigger scene]], physical decay becomes the ultimate truth of human existence. Shakespeare ties moral decay into biological reality, and Hamlet completes his philosophical descent by proving that all ambition, [[power]], and intellect end in anonymous dirt.

### Who & Where
- **[[Claudius]]** is the primary source of political corruption (and don't forget the other forms, say, murdering your siblings). He initiates the rot through his secret crime in the orchard, and as if that's not enough, sustains it through his covert espionage ([[eavesdropping]]) and later schemes to literally poison more people.
- **[[Hamlet]]** is the diagnoses this rot, and refuses to let the court ignore the stench (or at least tries to). He relentlessly injects imagery of maggots, ulcers, and worms, into conversations in the lobby (*see* [[fishmonger scene]]), his mother's private closet, and the Elsinore graveyard ([[gravedigger scene]]).
- This motif begins in the cold battlements where it is first pointed out, shows up in Gertrude's bedchamber (where Hamlet exposes her spiritual ulceration), and ends up in the graveyard (biological decay equalizes all human status).

### Connection to Deeper Themes
- **[[Appearance vs Reality]]:** A key mechanism of corruption is its operation under a rug where no one can see it. Here, the rug is a polished surface. Namely Claudius's smiling, capable monarchic exterior. Underneath, the nation rots from within, mirroring Hamlet's image of an ulcer topped off with some Neosporin and a band-aid.
- **[[Death & Mortality]]:** **So** much imagery of maggots, worms, and dust. Moral sin becomes biology here, as spiritual corruption in life is shown through literal decomposition after death.
- **[[Power]]:** Seized illegitimately, poisons the entire body politic, all of family, romance, justice, and diplomacy succumb to the infectious rot. (*see* [[revenge vs justice]])
