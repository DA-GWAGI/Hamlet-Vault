What happens when you realize you are entirely alone in standing against a dishonest, corrupt, and spying court? Psychological and physical alienation!

### Chronological Development
[[Hamlet]] begins the play visually and emotionally isolated from the rest of Denmark, as seen in his first entrance during the [[first court scene]]. While the entire court wears bright celebratory colors for [[Claudius]]'s wedding, Hamlet is brooding in his *"inky cloak"* and *"customary suits of solemn black"* (*see* [[grief]]). He also refuses to participate in their debaucherous partying, because it's effectively a collective profanation of his father's death (*see* [[death & mortality]]).

His isolation then deepens irreversibly when he meets [[the ghost]] of his dead father in the [[ghost's appearance]]. It binds him to a solitary blood oath, and Hamlet forces Horatio and Marcellus to never speak of or reveal what they saw. From here on out, he cuts himself off from institutional justice, believing the only way is to take upon the lonely burden of setting a broken world "right".

Across Acts 2 and 3, the members of Elsinore systematically strip away every remaining human connection to Hamlet. In Act 2, Scene 2, he welcomes [[Rosencrantz & Guildenstern]] as *"excellent good friends"* while immediately realizing they were sent for by Claudius as state informants (treacherous spies). Additionally, [[Ophelia]] obeys her father by returning Hamlet's love tokens, and in the [[nunnery scene]], she acts as the bait for a hidden [[eavesdropping]] scheme. His relationship with his mother has also fractured into hostility, and their confrontation at the end of Act 3 shows they no longer inhabit the same moral or perceptual reality (Hamlet sees the ghost, [[Gertrude]] does not, raising questions about [[appearance vs reality]]). These two acts conclude with his friendships, romantic bond, and remaining familial ties completely severed.

Now in Act 4, Hamlet's isolation takes the form of literal physical exile. Claudius ships him (under guard by his traitor ex-friends) to England for execution. Meanwhile, back in Elsinore, Ophelia experiences a parallel and fatal isolation. [[Polonius]] is dead, Laertes is in France, and Hamlet is exiled, leaving her with no allies to support or protect her. She proceeds to rapidly and inexorably descend into solitary [[madness]], verbally attack the royal members of court (condemning them for hypocrisy and wicked deceit), and then drowns alone in a weeping brook (*see* [[Ophelia's descent]] and [[Ophelia's flowers]]). This concludes Act 4. Don't worry, there's a cherry on top. In Act 5, moments before Hamlet dies (in [[the duel]]), he achieves a brief moment of true human solidarity with [[Horatio]] (that one guy who's apparently immune to court [[corruption]]). And then he dies in Horatio's arms, begging him to *"absent thee from felicity awhile"* so someone can live to tell Hamlet's true story (sorry, the cherry was [[poison]]). 

### Who & Where
- **[[Hamlet]]** is the primary target of isolation in this play. You know, his superior moral awareness is an alienating thing. Of course, as if his situation isn't enough to suit his teenage emo angst aesthetic, he also deepens his own solitude by adopting an antic disposition and pushing Ophelia away.
- **[[Claudius]]** and **[[Polonius]]** team up to actively engineer Hamlet’s isolation, co-opting his friends and lover into spying on him.
- **[[Ophelia]]** suffers enforced patriarchal isolation ([[misogyny]]). This is shown first in her family quarters, and then in the main hall. She tragically succumbs to madness and finds herself unraveling alone at the brook, where no one intervenes to save her.

### Connection to Deeper Themes
- **[[Existentialism]]:** What is left when you are stripped of reliable family, friends, and political institutions? You'll have to find out yourself by confronting mortality and meaning entirely alone. Have fun exploring the prison that is your own consciousness.
- **[[Loyalty]]:** The betrayal of friendship and romance for Hamlet is universal, but not without a caveat. Horatio's incorruptible loyalty is a clear moral anchor for the play.
- **[[Action vs Inaction]]:** Isolation literally means you have no one, so it's no surprise Hamlet can't trust anyone to share his burden with or verify his suspicions. Carrying the entire moral weight of Claudius's bizarre crime alone surely won't magnify hesitation.
