How we all feel about the blurring line between Hamlet's tactical performance of insanity and Ophelia's literal psychological collapse.

### Chronological Development
Madness is the only motif first introduced in the play as a calculated survival tactic (though up to debate, *see* [[performance]]). However, it quickly spirals into psychological trauma. After receiving the hit order on [[Claudius]] (*see* [[the ghost]], [[ghost's appearance]]), [[Hamlet]] swears his friends to secrecy and puts on an *"antic disposition"*. He intends to use eccentric (or erratic, rather) behavior as camouflage to investigate his murder victim (*see* [[appearance vs reality]]). In Act 2, Scene 1, Ophelia describes Hamlet bursting into her chamber pale, unbraced, and trembling, prompting [[Polonius]] to misdiagnose him with *"the very ecstasy of love."* In the next scene, Hamlet maintains control over his feigned madness (though who knows how long this control will last). He delivers a slew of double entendres in the [[fishmonger scene]] (*see* [[puns]]) that cause Polonius to remark in an [[aside]], *"Though this be madness, yet there is method in 't."*

Boundaries between performance and losing emotional control begin to erode in Act 3. After Hamlet lashes out at Ophelia in the [[nunnery scene]], she laments seeing Hamlet’s *"noble and most sovereign reason, / Like sweet bells jangled, out of tune and harsh."* This is a strange comparison of her lover's sharp mind and deteriorated consciousness to a pleasant harmony of bells now all out of tune and dissonant. Leading up to the [[death of Polonius]] in [[Gertrude]]'s closet, Hamlet insists to his mother that he is *"essentially not in madness, / But mad in craft"*. Then minutes later he impulsively slaughters an innocent guy (well, I mean, at least in context) through a curtain and talks to his imaginary friend. Like any normal person would conclude, Gertrude interprets his vision of the ghost as *"the very coinage of your brain."*

In Act 4, Hamlet's theatrical madness finds an unambiguous [[foil]] in [[Ophelia]]'s authentic psychological collapse (*see* [[Ophelia's descent]]. This seems inevitable after her father dies a violent death at the hands of her ex-lover. Ophelia exhibits this madness when she enters public court singing fragmented ballads and distributing (imaginary?) flowers (though they are symbolic, she's roasting them! *see* [[Ophelia's flowers]]). She seems to be speaking *"things in doubt, / That carry but half sense,"* yet her comments expose raw truths about [[grief]], betrayal, and guilt. She manages to voice things that, if not excused by her madness, would warrant execution. We get to see Hamlet lose control again in Act 5, Scene 1, when he leaps into Ophelia's open grave and grapples with Laertes (in a fit of *"towering passion"*). He later apologizes to Laertes right before their fencing match ([[the duel]]), although it kind of sounds more like an excuse than a sincere apology. He attributes his past cruelty to his "madness," managing to self-victimize by framing his fractured mind as a separate enemy that wronged them both.

### Who & Where
- **[[Hamlet]]** fakes insanity as a political shield and satirical weapon in the court lobbies, but he's not that good of an actor. By the time he's confronting Gertrude in her bedchamber, he's seemingly lost it.
- **[[Ophelia]]** embodies tragic madness induced by loss and [[isolation]]. Her grief deactivates repression, and she goes through the main hall denouncing court [[corruption]] before wandering into the weeping brook.
- **[[Polonius]]** and **[[Claudius]]** weasel around the court, trying to interpret madness because they believe finding its root cause will neutralize Hamlet.

### Connection to Deeper Themes
- **[[Appearance vs Reality]]:** Shakespeare makes it unclear as to whether Hamlet is just acting insane or actually becoming unhinged. Perhaps wearing a mask long enough will inevitably alter the face underneath.
- **[[Grief]]:** Hamlet intellectualizes and weaponizes his grief throughout the play, the antic disposition is an example. On the other hand, Ophelia's unprocessed grief breaks her psyche completely and eventually leads to her drowning.
- **[[Power]]:** In a tyrannical court, madness paradoxically provides these people freedom. Both Hamlet's wordplay and Ophelia's songs allow them to indict Claudius's regime without risking immediate execution.
