Titular character of the play. Son of Gertrude and recently deceased King Hamlet. A grieving prince entrapped in philosophical paralysis after his late father's ghost demands justice.

### Psychological Arc
Hamlet begins the play in a state of profound [[grief]] and acute, isolating depression. We meet him in the [[first court scene]], delivering a [[soliloquy]] about his disgust with the moral cheapness and [[corruption]] of Elsinore. The speech shows his self-destructive tendencies even before his meeting with [[the ghost]], wishing his *"too, too sullied flesh would melt."* After the [[ghost's appearance]], where vengeance is demanded, Hamlet intentionally splits his psyche between an inner world and an *"antic disposition"*, hoping this will buy him time to investigate. An amusing instance of this is his mockery of [[Polonius]] in the [[fishmonger scene]]. 

He then descends into an epistemological loop of overthinking, convinced he needs absolute certainty before acting. In an attempt to determine [[Claudius]]'s guilt, he sets up a play-within-a-play to recreate the events of his father's murder ([[mousetrap]]).

Across Acts 2 and 3, his hesitation unravels into self-loathing (*"O, what a rogue and peasant slave am I!"*), suicidal ideation, and existential gridlock ("[[To be, or not to be]]"). Although no single turning point or event seems capable of ripping him out of this mental paralysis, a series of messy shocks nudges him to face the inevitable deed he must do. First, he impulsively stabs an arras after hearing a cry for help behind it (thinking it was Claudius), resulting in the [[death of Polonius]]. Later, he discovers Claudius's hit order on the ship to England (and changes it to execute [[Rosencrantz & Guildenstern]] instead). Through this and the rest, Hamlet's manic over-intellectualization begins to burn away.

In Act 5, Hamlet finally reaches a point of fatalistic acceptance in the [[gravedigger scene]] while standing in the graveyard and holding [[Yorick's skull]]. In the short remainder of the play, he stops trying to engineer cosmic justice and just embraces what happens with a sort of stoic readiness (*"The readiness is all"*). 

### Key Relationships
- **[[Claudius]]:** Hamlet's uncle and stepfather. They have a strange dynamic of cold rivalry (hatred) and mutual surveillance. Claudius may be morally bankrupt, but he is smooth, decisive, and pragmatic. Hamlet embodies conscience, but his perspective is destabilized. Neither can strike the other without losing political or spiritual ground until the mousetrap exposes Claudius' guilt.
- **[[Gertrude]]:** Hamlet's mother's remarriage is arguably his deepest emotional wound, even above his father's murder. Viewing it as an emotional betrayal fueled by cruel and selfish desires, his anger spirals into misogyny, later saying *"Frailty, thy name is woman!"*. His obsession with her marriage spurs his developing disgust with humanity and drives their confrontation in her closet, where Hamlet urges for her repentance and support.
- **[[Ophelia]]:** Before the play begins, they had genuine affection for each other. However, as the events unfold, Hamlet's distrust of Gertrude (and broader hatred of women) bleeds into his treatment of Ophelia. She becomes collateral damage as he realizes she is being used as a pawn by Polonius and Claudius (and thus cannot be trusted), subjecting her to cruelty in order to sever their bond. He tells her to *"get thee to a nunnery"* (in the [[nunnery scene]]), denies ever loving her, and accuses her of deceit. This plays a significant part in [[Ophelia's descent]].
- **[[Horatio]]:** Hamlet's anchor to sanity and objective reality. Horatio tells it as it is and is *"not passion's slave"* (not ruled by emotions or flattery, remains rational). Because of this, Hamlet trusts him exclusively with his confrontation with the ghost and his mousetrap scheme. Ultimately, he relies on Horatio to survive the chaos (in [[the duel]]) and restore Hamlet's reputation after the bloodbath that ensues.
- **[[Laertes]]:** He reacts to his father's murder with immediate and unthinking fury, willing to do whatever it takes to get revenge. This blind rage is a [[foil]] to Hamlet's excessively contemplative approach.
- **[[Fortinbras]]:** Another structural foil, he marches an army across borders for a worthless patch of land ([[Fortinbras' troops]]). This prompts Hamlet's shameful reflection over his inaction despite urgency.

### Pivotal Moments
- **The [[First Court Scene]] (Act 1, Scene 2):** A core tension between introspective truth and outward [[performance]] is established, Hamlet's psychological baseline is suicidal and despairing, Denmark is decaying
- **The [[Ghost's Appearance]] (Act 1, Scene 5):** Hamlet's worldview is shattered after learning of the nature of his father's death, prompting him to shun trivial knowledge and develop a fragmented psyche (manifesting in feigned [[madness]])
- **The [[Fishmonger Scene]] (Act 2, Scene 2):** The first active deployment of Hamlet's antic disposition, where he uses [[puns]] to mock Polonius's meddling and probe Elsinore's surveillance
- **To be, or not to be (Act 3, Scene 1):** Full on existential meditation of suicidal and conscience, abstract and epistemological
- **The [[Nunnery Scene]] (Act 3, Scene 1):** Contrast immediately after philosophical soliloquy, where Hamlet viciously attacks Ophelia (and probably detects Claudius and Polonius)
- **The [[Mousetrap]] & Prayer Scene (Act 3, Scenes 2-3):** Hamlet stages the murder and confirms Claudius's guilt beyond doubt, then minutes later spares him because killing Claudius mid-prayer might send him to heaven (showing over-scrupulous nature)
- **The [[Death of Polonius]] (Act 3, Scene 4):** A single blind, unthinking action kills the wrong guy: destroying Ophelia, angering/radicalizing Laertes, and giving Claudius reason to give him the boot (likely reinforcing Hamlet to overthink)
- **The Graveyard & [[The Duel]] (Act 5, Scenes 1-2):** Holding Yorick's skull equalizes any human ambition or caution in Hamlet's mind, leaving him with cool rationality and a resignation to fate

### Associated Concepts
##### Themes
- **[[Action vs Inaction]]:** Hamlet's defining struggle between conscience and impulse
- **[[Appearance vs Reality]]:** One must navigate a court of spies and liars (by feigning madness, in this instance)
- **[[Existentialism]] and [[Death & Mortality]]:** Fixation on suicide, cosmic meaning, decay
- **[[Certainty & Truth]]:** Demanding proof when it might as well be an impossibility
- **[[Revenge vs Justice]]:** When getting revenge for your father might just damn your own soul
##### Motifs
- **[[Madness]] and [[Performance]]:** Are you still just performing insanity or actually losing control...
- **[[Misogyny]]:** Hamlet is Projecting Gertrude's betrayal onto all women
- **[[Isolation]]:** Standing completely alone in grief against a corrupt and debaucherous state
- **[[Eavesdropping]]:** Constantly being hunted by Polonius, Claudius, and Rosencrantz & Guildenstern
##### Symbols
- **[[Yorick's Skull]]:** The physical catalyst that snaps Hamlet out of it, and shifts his attitude towards acceptance (of mortality)
- **[[The Ghost]]:** The unresolved past haunts the present (and Hamlet's conscience)
##### Literary Devices
- **[[Soliloquy]]:** The primary vehicle tracking Hamlet's psychological shifts
- **[[Puns]]:** Hamlet's weapon against his haters in court
- **[[Foil]]:** He's directly contrasted against Laertes and Fortinbras

### Important Quotes
- *Seems, madam? Nay, it is. I know not 'seems.* (Act 1, Scene 2): Establishes a tension between being and seeming. Gertrude implies Hamlet is only performing such extreme grief and should move on, to which Hamlet responds he actually feels this way. The rest of court may as well be performing with their shallow theatrical mourning though.
- *The time is out of joint. O cursed spite / That ever I was born to set it right!* (Act 1, Scene 5): He immediately resents the moral burden placed on him, which shows his reluctance to carry out vengeance (as opposed to some of these people's eagerness)
- *"To be, or not to be—that is the question..."* (Act 3, Scene 1): This is the heart. Human consciousness and fear of the unknown make cowards of us all. Also, even urgent moral duties requiring grand action will make us turn inward into paralysis.
- *"Get thee to a nunnery."* (Act 3, Scene 1): His disgust with Gertrude's sexuality and paranoia over court espionage manifest in cruelty toward Ophelia.
- *"Now might I do it pat, now he is a-praying..."* (Act 3, Scene 3): Hamlet's fatal flaw in action here. He turns down an easy kill because he's too concerned with engineering Claudius's eternal damnation. 
- *"How all occasions do inform against me / And spur my dull revenge!"* (Act 4, Scene 4): Hamlet's final soliloquy. Watching Fortinbras's army forces him to confront his own overthinking.
- *"There is special providence in the fall of a sparrow... The readiness is all."* (Act 5, Scene 2): Hamlet's psychological arc finally comes to a conclusion. He surrenders his obsessive control to a peaceful acceptance of mortality.
